    \section{Design Task Definition}

        The objective of the design is to design a lorry crane, that can operative with a given mass over a defined drive cycle, and withstand a worst-case loading scenario.\\
        For the task a certain values were given, mainly defining the drive cycle. Some values were true for all group, while others were group specific.

        \subsection{General Values}

            The general values for the drive-cycle provided included:\\
            \begin{itemize}
                \item $v_p01$ $\rightarrow$ Normalised velocity vector for piston 1
                \item $v_p02$ $\rightarrow$ Normalised velocity vector for piston 2
                \item $e_1$ $\rightarrow$ Level-arm for cylinder 1
                \item $e_2$ $\rightarrow$ Level-arm for cylinder 2
                \item $\theta_1$ $\rightarrow$ First arm angle
                \item $\theta_2$ $\rightarrow$ Second arm angle
                \item $L_0$ $\rightarrow$ Base height for lorry crane
                \item $p_2$ $\rightarrow$ Pressure on low-pressure side
                \item $\eta$ $\rightarrow$ Oil dynamic viscosity
                \item $c_v$ $\rightarrow$ Laminar leakage loss coefficient
                \item $k_p$ $\rightarrow$ Coloumb friction coefficient
                \item $k_v$ $\rightarrow$ Viscous friction loss coefficient
                \item $k_{\epsilon}$ $\rightarrow$ Displacement coefficient
            \end{itemize}
            The information on the work-case scenario, provided the following values:
            \begin{center}
                \begin{tabular}{cccc}
                    \hline
                    Parameter & & Cylinder 1 & Cylinder 2\\
                    \hline
                    $\theta_{1,wc} [\text{rad}]$ & & 1,2 & 0,35\\
                    $\theta_{2,wc} [\text{rad}]$ & & 0,0  & 0,0\\
                    $e_{1,wc} [\text{m}]$ & & 0,14 & 0,48\\
                    $e_{2,wc} [\text{m}]$ & & 0.57 & 0,17\\
                    \hline
                \end{tabular}
            \end{center}
         
        \subsection{Group-specific values}
            There were many group-specific values that we were given for the design task, these included:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item $M_{t,wc}$ $\rightarrow$ Maximum total load mass
                \item $q_{r,wc}$ $\rightarrow$ Worst-case flow to drive-cycle-max flow ratio
                \item $M_t$ $\rightarrow$ Total load mass
                \item $L_1$ $\rightarrow$ First arm length
                \item $L_2$ $\rightarrow$ Second arm length
                \item $v_{p0}$ $\rightarrow$ Reference cylinder velocity
                \item $t_0$ $\rightarrow$ Drive-cycle Time
                \item $P_{s,max}$ $\rightarrow$ Maximum system pressure
                \item $\eta_c$ $\rightarrow$ Cylinder Efficiency
            \end{itemize}    
            
        \subsection{Denormalisation and discretization}

            From the provided values we can see that the velocity profile which we were provided has been normalised and we have been provided a reference value.
            Using this reference value we denormalised the normalised velocity profile, to determine the effective velocity profile of the two cylinders.
            \begin{align*}
                v_{p1} &= v_{p0} \cdot v_{p01}\\
                v_{p2} &= v_{p1} \cdot v_{p02}
            \end{align*}
            Additionally, we discretized time to a number of discrete times equal to the length of the provided vectors for velocity, angles and arms, as such:
            \begin{lstlisting}[language=Octave]
time = linspace(0,t_0,length(e_1));
            \end{lstlisting}

        \subsection{Cylinder Drive-cycle}
            From here we continued by determining the forces on the two cylinders throughout the drive-cycle. We did this by calculating the moment generated on either arm hinge by the respective cylinder and the mass at the crane tip.
            We determined the resulting forces to have the equations:
            \begin{align*}
                F_1 &= M_t\cdot g\cdot \frac{(L_1\cdot\cos(\theta_1) + L_2\cdot\cos(\theta_2))}{e_1}\\
                F_2 &= M_t\cdot g\cdot \frac{L_2\cdot\cos(\theta_2)}{e_2}
            \end{align*}
            The equivalent MATLAB code is as follows:
            \begin{lstlisting}[language=Octave]
F_1 = M_t*g*(L_1*cos(th_1)+L_2*cos(th_2))./e_1;
F_2 = M_t*g*L_2*cos(th_2)./e_2;
            \end{lstlisting}
            With $F_1$ and $F_2$ determined, we could now plot how they changed through time.
            \begin{figure}[!ht]
                \centering

                \caption{Forces on the two cylinders in the system}

        \subsection{Worst-case Scenario and Cylinder Diameter}
            Using the same equations we used to determine the forces during the drive-cycle, we can find the forces in the worst-case scenario for the system, with the MATLAB code as follows:
            \begin{lstlisting}
F_wc_1 = M_tmax*g*(L_1*cos(th_1_wc_cyl1)+L_2*cos(th_2_wc_cyl1))./e_1_wc_cyl1;
F_wc_2 = M_tmax*g*L_2*cos(th_2_wc_cyl2)./e_2_wc_cyl2;
            \end{lstlisting}
            In this case the $\theta$ and $e$ values for either cylinder during the drive-cycle were replaced by the given worst-case scenario values.
            From these forces we could determine the diameter of the cylinder required to withstand these inopportune forces.
            We calculated the area and diameter required as:
            \begin{align*}
                A_1 &= \frac{F_{1,wc}}{\eta_c \cdot (p_{1,max}-0.5p_2)}\\
                D_1 &= \sqrt{\frac{4*A_1}{\pi}}\\
                A_1 &= \frac{F_{2,wc}}{\eta_c \cdot (p_{1,max}-0.5p_2)}\\
                D_1 &= \sqrt{\frac{4*A_2}{\pi}}
            \end{align*}
            In which $p_{1,max} = p_{s,max} - p_{extra}$, where $p_{extra}$ is the unavoidable losses in the system, which he have to account for and which we arbitrarily determined to be $p_{extra} \approx 2000000 Pa$.
            We determined the following values for the respective variables:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item $A_1 = 0.020437$ $m^2$
                \item $D_1 = 0.1613$ $m$
                \item $A_2 = 0.014064$ $m^2$
                \item $D_2 = 0.1338$ $m$
            \end{itemize}
            Because no manufacturer produces cylinder of every size, we need to determine the cylinder with diameter which fits our needs.
            To do this we looked through \textit{Bosch Rexroth's} linear hydraulic cylinder catalogue, and found a suitable candidate.
            Out of the catalogue we ended up choosing a two Series CGH3 type hydralic cylinders. These cylinders are mill-type, double acting and can withstand the requirement maximum pressure.
            Of the CGH3 cylinder options we chose a cylinder with a diameter of $180 mm$ and for the second cylinder a diameter of $140mm$. In total the following data was determined necessary:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item Cylinder 1 Bore Diameter: $D_1 = 0.18$ $m$
                \item Cylinder 2 Bore Diamter: $D_2 = 0.14$ $m$
                \item Cylinder 1 Area on Piston Side: $A_{p1} = 0.025447$ $m^2$
                \item Cylinder 1 Ring Area on Rod Side: $A_{r1} = 0.013175$ $m^2$
                \item Cylinder 2 Area on Piston Side: $A_{p2} = 0.015394$ $m^2$
                \item Cylinder 2 Ring Area on Rod Side: $A_{r2} = 0.007540$ $m^2$

            To be absolutely sure we made the right choice we also found the maximum stroke velocities for the respective cylinders and determined them to be:
            \begin{itemize}

    \section{Circuit Drive-cycle Analysis}

        \subsection{System Pressure}
        For the drive-cycle analysis we need to determine the pressures and flow our cylinders operate at; this later help us determine what type of system hydraulic circuit we are interested in using.
        We know that the pressure on the low-pressure side will always be $1 MPa$, and since we know that the sign of the force never changes on either cylinder, we know that the rod side of the cylinder will always be low-pressure side. Because of this we use one single value for $p_2$ for both cylinders.
        The equation to determine the pressure on the high pressure side of the cylinder depends on whether the cylinder is acting as a pump or a motor.
        When the cylinder is operating as a pump the equation is:
        \begin{equation*}
            p_1 = \frac{p_2\cdot A_2 + \frac{F}{\eta_c}}{A_1}
        \end{equation*}
        And when the cylinder is operating as a motor, the equation is:
        \begin{equation*}
            p_1 = \frac{p_2\cdot A_2 + \eta_c\cdot F}{A_1}
        \end{equation*}
        Since the only difference is how the efficiency changes the resulting force, in MATLAB we can treat this equation as single equation, where $\eta_c$ changes definition based on the sign of the velocity.\\
        \begin{lstlisting}
v_1_pos = v_p1 > 0;
v_1_neg = v_p1 < 0;
v_1_stat = v_p1 == 0;
eta_c1 = v_1_pos./eta_c + v_1_neg*eta_c + v_1_stat;
v_2_pos = v_p2 > 0;
v_2_neg = v_p2 < 0;
v_2_stat = v_p2 == 0;
eta_c2 = v_2_pos./eta_c + v_2_neg*eta_c + v_2_stat;

p_2 = p_2*ones(1,length(time));
p_1_cyl1 = (F_1./eta_c1 + p_2*A_r1)/(A_p1);
p_1_cyl2 = (p_2*A_r2 + F_2./eta_c2)/(A_p2);
        \end{lstlisting}
        We can also determine the minimum pressure our a constant-pressure pump would have to operate at for the system to function as:\\
        \begin{lstlisting}
p_1max_cyl1 = max(p_1_cyl1);
p_1max_cyl2 = max(p_1_cyl2);
p_min = max([p_1max_cyl1 p_1max_cyl2]) * ones(1,length(time));
        \end{lstlisting}

        From these computations we can plot the graph of pressure for the cycle and minimum pressure for the system.

        \subsection{System Flow}

        To determine the flow we also need to consider the sign which the velocity takes during the drive-cycle, as we have to change which chamber the flow moves to.
        The resulting MATLAB code is as follows.\\
        \begin{lstlisting}
q_1 = A_p1*v_p1.*v_1_pos + A_r1*abs(v_p1).*v_1_neg;
q_2 = A_p2*v_p2.*v_2_pos + A_r1*abs(v_p2).*v_2_neg;
q_tot = q_1 + q_2;
q_max = (max(q_tot)/q_rwc)*ones(1,length(time));
        \end{lstlisting}
        The equation for $q_{max}$ came from the definition for the worst-case scenario flow ratio, and is the worst-case level of flow that our pump must be able to handle.

        \subsection{Hydraulic System Type and System Diagram}
            After our analysis of the system pressure and flow, we had to decide what type of circuit our hydraulic system would have.
            We decided that our hydraulic circuit should be a constant pressure system with variable flow. We made this choice because as can be seen in the respective plots, the pressure is relatively constant
            when compared to the flow which goes from $~0.0016 m^3/s$ to zero. Since all of the flow during the period where the velocity is zero would be wasted, we believed that the best way to reduce our losses would be to employ this system.
            We also considered that a more efficient system to reduce the losses further would be a load-sensing system with variable flow. But considering these types of system are even more expensive than the constant-pressure system, we decided
            to opt for a constant-pressure system to keep that system cost to a more middling cost.
            Finally the drivability of the system is relatively good, because it exhibit load dependency, which adds high damping to our system making it more drivable. The system also doesn't exhibit load interference which would reduce the drivability.

    \section{Pump Selection}

        For pump selection we decided to turn once again to the catalogue of Bosch Rexroth.
        Looking through their catalogue we found a pump that seemed to fit our requirement. This pump is a A10VZO size 45 pump.
        This pump has the following parameters:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Maximum Displacement per Revolution, $V_{g,max}$ = $45$ $\text{cm}^3/\text{rev}$
            \item Maximum Flow (at $n_{nom} \text{ and } V_{g,max}$) $q_v$ = $135$ [$\text{lpm}$]
            \item Maximum Rotational Speed, $n_{nom}$ = $3000$ rpm
            \item Maximum Pressure, $P_{max}$ = $315$ bar
            \item Nominal Pressure, $P_{nom}$ = $250$ bar
        \end{itemize}
        The pump can operate up to it's maximum rotational speed in either direction. But when the rotational velocity is positive (when it's operating as a pump), and under $200$ rpm, the operating period can only be below $3$ minuntes, if the displacemet is $V_g \leq V_{g,max}$
        The volumetric flow can be calculated with the following equation:
        \begin{equation*}
            q_v = \frac{V_{g,max}\cdot n \cdot \eta_{vol}}{1000} \text{  [lpm]}
        \end{equation*}

        \subsection{Displacement Coefficient and Efficiencies}

            To determine the volumetric and hydro-mechanical efficiency we need to find the displacement coefficient for the pump.
            To determine this coefficient we used the flow equation from the formula book.
            \begin{equation*}
                q_{ep} = \varepsilon \cdot D_p \cdot n \cdot \eta_{vol,p}
            \end{equation*}
            The problem now was that we did not have some value, so we relied on some assumptions to determine these values.
            The first assumption is that $D_p = V_{g,max}$, additionally we also assumed that $n = 2500$ [rpm], and finally we assumed that $q_{ep} = q_{tot}$.
            Since we are determining the volumetric efficiency from this value, we couldn't assume a value, so what we decided to do was to plug in the equation for the efficiency itself:
            \begin{equation*}
                \eta_{vol,p} = 1 - C_v \cdot \frac{\Delta p}{|\varepsilon_p|\cdot n_p\cdot \eta}
            \end{equation*}
            Plugging in this equation and isolating $\varepsilon_p$, we got the following equation:
            \begin{equation*}
                \varepsilon_p = \frac{q_{pump} + V_{g,max}\cdot C_v \cdot p_{pump} / \eta}{V_{g,max}\cdot n}
            \end{equation*}
            \begin{figure}[!ht]
                \centering
                \includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{Plots/DisplacementCoefficient.png}\\
                \caption{Displacment Coefficient}
            \end{figure}

            For the volumetric and hydro-mechanical we used the equations as indicated in the formula book, which we will report.
            \begin{equation*}
                \eta_{vol,p} = 1 - C_v\cdot\frac{\Delta p}{|\varepsilon_p|\cdot n_p \eta}
            \end{equation*}
            \begin{equation*}
                \eta_{hm,p} = \frac{1}{1 + (k_p\cdot k_v\frac{n_p\eta}{\Delta p})\cdot e^{(k_{\varepsilon}(1-|\varepsilon_p|))}}
            \end{equation*}
            And the total efficiency is obviously product of the two efficiencies:
            \begin{equation*}
                \eta_{tot} = \eta_{vol} \cdot \eta_{hm}
            \end{equation*}
            \begin{figure}[!ht]
                \centering
                \includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{Plots/EfficienciesPlot.png}\\
                \caption{Volumetric, Hydro-mechanical and Total Efficiency}
            \end{figure}

            From the values we found in the section we were able to determine the hydraluc input power:
            \begin{equation*}
                P_{in} = p_{pump} \cdot q_{tot}
            \end{equation*}
            We also determined the output power with the following MATLAB code:
            \begin{lstlisting}
P_out_1 = (v_1_pos.*v_p1.*F_1 + v_1_neg .* q_1 .* p_2 * eta_c);
P_out_2 = (v_2_pos.*v_p2.*F_2 + v_2_neg .* q_2 .* p_2 * eta_c);
P_out = P_out_1 + P_out_2;
            \end{lstlisting}
            We determined that while the cylinder acted as a pump, the mechanical output power was equal to $v\cdot F$, but when the cylinder acted as a pump we had to consider that we were supplying the low-pressure side alone, so we determined that the output power would be $q\cdot p\cdot \eta_c$.
            The power graph became the following:
            \begin{figure}[!ht]
                \centering
                \includegraphics[width = 0.8\textwidth]{Plots/PowerPlot.png}\\
                \caption{Input and Output Power for System}
            \end{figure}
            \clearpage
            With the resulting efficiency being determined by the fraction of input power that becomes output power.
            \begin{equation*}
                \eta_{\text{sys}} = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}}
            \end{equation*}
            \begin{figure}[!ht]
                \centering
                \includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{Plots/SystemEfficiency.png}\\
                \caption{Input and Output Power for System}
            \end{figure}
            The efficiency is high when both cylinders are acting as pumps since the pressure required is high, but very low when they are both acting as motors.
            The highest efficiency is achieved when the required flow is relatively low, and the pressure is near the maximum required, so the losses are at their lowest.

            The efficiency is relatively when compared to other systems such as a load-sensing system. If we had used a load-sensing system we would have higher efficiency, and probably better drivability, but the cost would also greater. Whereas in term of efficiency the constant flow system would probably have lower efficiency in the first-half, but higher efficiency on the second-half.
            We would not recommend our system because of the very low efficiencies on the return-stroke that also require more expenses on the cooler.
